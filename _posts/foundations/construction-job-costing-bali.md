A contractor renovating a villa in Bali gets a progress certificate on Thursday. The draw clears part of an advance, the client holds back some retention, and the electrician needs paying before Friday. The bank transfer might only arrive the following week. If the owner opens the project report and asks whether the job is profitable, each of those events answers a different part of the question. The site team needs to know what work has been done. The project manager needs the cost to finish it. The finance team needs to know what can be billed and when the cash is likely to move.

Construction job costing brings those answers together by project. A contractor can use the same process on a villa renovation, a restaurant fit-out or several jobs running at once. This guide follows a contractor hired to deliver work for a client. The client’s own records have a separate purpose and support decisions such as which improvement costs belong with a property and how those costs were funded. ## Open a job record before ordering anything

Give each signed job a project code and record the client entity, site, contract currency, starting price and billable milestones. Save the signed scope and schedule of values with the budget. The schedule of values allocates the contract sum across packages or milestones so there is a basis for measuring and billing work. Record the person authorised to approve changes and the evidence the contract requires for a claim. If the job came through an architect or a property manager, confirm who signs certificates and which legal entity receives the invoice.

Indonesia’s Construction Services Law requires the contract to set out the method of payment, including the client’s payment obligations and payment security. Use the signed payment clause to put measurement dates, sign-offs, invoice due dates and retention release events on the project calendar. A draw may need a signed valuation before the contractor can submit an invoice. Record how many days the contract allows for each step and give someone responsibility for following up when a step runs late. [Indonesia’s Construction Services Law, Article 47(1)(f)](https://berkas.dpr.go.id/puspanlakuu/kompilasi/kompilasi-public-400.pdf#page=40)

Add a short set of cost codes that the site and accounts teams will recognise. For a villa renovation, these might cover demolition, structure, plumbing and electrical, finishes, site labour and shared site costs. Give each line a budget and, where possible, a quantity and unit price. Add the codes to orders, timesheets, delivery records, subcontractor claims and reimbursements. Agree how to split a truckload delivered to two jobs or a supervisor’s time across several sites. Record the allocation basis so both project managers can check their figures.

Preserve the original budget and maintain a dated revision with approvals. This lets the reviewer see whether a change came from selling extra work, accepting a higher supplier quote or correcting an estimate. If the quantity surveyor revises the electrical budget after opening up a wall, keep the original scope and the survey notes. At the next review, show what changed, which part is covered by a client variation, and which part the contractor expects to absorb. A site estimate is useful even while the client approval is pending.

Identify which materials and fees the client buys directly. A client might select and pay a tile supplier while the contractor provides installation. In that case the job budget needs the contractor’s labour, adhesives, handling and any agreed responsibility for breakage. Keep the client order reference if it affects delivery or acceptance. If the contractor orders on the client’s behalf and expects reimbursement, record the agreement, proof of purchase and settlement separately for review. Making the purchaser and party responsible for each charge clear helps the site team resolve shortages and lets finance explain which amounts belong in the contractor’s ledger.

## Tie orders and material receipts to the cost forecast

Maintain a commitment register for open purchase orders and subcontracts. Record the supplier, order amount, site, cost code, quantities, expected delivery and unpaid portion. When goods arrive or a subcontractor finishes a stage, match the delivery or acceptance evidence to the order and invoice. Update the outstanding commitment for the portion delivered so the same amount appears once in your forecast. A purchasing deposit may need its own record until the goods or services arrive. Link it to the order so accounts can apply it to the correct final bill.

At month-end, ask site leads about materials received and services accepted without an invoice. Record the receipt or measurement, expected cost and who can confirm it. Your accountant can review whether a cut-off entry is required on the accounting basis you use. When the invoice arrives, match it to the earlier record and clear any accrual as appropriate. Show materials held for a later phase separately in the job file. Where the finance team measures progress from costs, review unused material, abnormal waste and significant equipment purchases so the measure reflects the work performed. [IFRS 15, paragraphs B18–B19](https://www.ifrs.org/content/dam/ifrs/publications/html-standards/english/2026/issued/ifrs15.html)

Reforecast costs with the people who know the remaining quantities and lead times. Start with recorded job costs to date, add outstanding commitments for work or deliveries still to come, then estimate remaining work that has yet to be ordered. Include the cost of remobilising after a pause or sending a team back to close a snag list if the project is likely to need it. Keep the basis of each material estimate. A note such as “two deliveries still needed at the revised supplier quote” gives a reviewer somewhere to start next month.

## Keep the variation log linked to the budget

Give each proposed variation a reference and note its scope, quote, likely cost, timing effect and approval status. An extra drainage run, for example, may require more trenching, materials and labour, plus a later inspection. It can be useful to record a site instruction immediately and send the written pricing and timing implications for approval before ordering. Keep correspondence and the contract’s approval evidence against the same reference. Confirm whether approved scope also changes a progress milestone or the basis on which an advance will be recovered.

Bring approved changes into the working contract schedule and cost forecast together. Keep provisional sums and disagreements visible in the review pack. If the team has already spent money on work awaiting a client decision, record the costs against the right job and keep the potential recovery visible as an open question. Have your accountant review any disputed or conditional amounts before using them in financial reporting. For internal decisions, showing a base forecast and a separately identified scenario for proposed changes helps the owner see how much margin depends on approvals.

## Reconcile progress, billing and receipts

Keep a dated measurement of work completed, supported by the contract’s agreed evidence. This might include a quantity surveyor’s valuation, signed inspection, handover checklist or a client’s approval of a milestone. A progress certificate records what has been approved for a claim under the agreement. Save the amount requested and the amount certified together. If a client certifies a lower quantity or defers approval of a package, explain the difference in the billing schedule. Follow the same line from certification through invoicing to receipts.

Maintain a cumulative schedule showing gross certified work, any advance recovered from the claim, retention, net amount payable and bank receipts. The schedule should tie to the claims and customer balances. Confirm the supporting payment records you need with whoever prepares your tax reports, particularly when an invoice and the net cash received differ. Preserve any applicable tax invoice, withholding evidence, payment confirmation, credit note and explanation against the job or invoice. The applicable handling depends on the company and transaction. [Lumara’s tax-ready records checklist](/blog/tax-and-reporting/tax-ready)

For financial reporting, agree how your company recognises revenue under its applicable accounting framework. Under Indonesia’s PSAK 115 model, revenue is recognised when or as the entity satisfies a performance obligation. Work that improves an asset the client controls while it is being improved can qualify for recognition over time. Where the criteria apply, the accountant uses a reliable measure of progress that reflects delivery and updates it at each reporting date. Keep the signed scope, changes, valuation evidence and supporting job costs together so the accountant can review timing and whether any balances represent receivables, contract assets or contract liabilities. [Indonesian Institute of Accountants guidance on PSAK 115](https://web.iaiglobal.or.id/assets/materi/Sertifikasi/CA/modul/pk_19/files/basic-html/page341.html) · [IFRS 15, paragraphs 31–45 and 105–108](https://www.ifrs.org/content/dam/ifrs/publications/html-standards/english/2026/issued/ifrs15.html)

### A villa renovation in rupiah

Assume a private renovation originally sold for IDR 1,200 million with a planned cost of IDR 900 million. An approved scope change adds IDR 60 million of selling price and IDR 40 million of expected cost. The revised contract value is IDR 1,260 million. The site team has recorded IDR 470 million in costs to date, including IDR 35 million of accepted subcontract work awaiting an invoice. It has IDR 210 million of outstanding commitments for future deliveries and work. It also forecasts IDR 260 million for the remaining work it has yet to order.

| Job forecast item | IDR million |
| --- | ---: |
| Revised contract value | 1,260 |
| Job costs to date | 470 |
| Outstanding commitments for future work | 210 |
| Estimated cost of remaining uncommitted work | 260 |
| Forecast final job cost | 940 |
| Forecast job margin before company overheads and tax | 320 |

The forecast final cost is IDR 940 million, giving a forecast job margin of IDR 320 million, or about 25.4% of the revised contract value. The project manager can compare that with the original IDR 300 million margin and see how much the approved variation contributes. The forecast still depends on the updated estimates. If the remaining work rises by IDR 30 million without a matching contract adjustment, the forecast job margin falls to IDR 290 million. The owner can use that sensitivity to decide which quotes or quantities to verify first.

Suppose the client has paid an IDR 126 million advance and the first progress certificate approves IDR 504 million of work. Under this contract, the first claim applies IDR 50.4 million of the advance and holds IDR 25.2 million as retention. The balance payable for the draw is IDR 428.4 million. The customer pays IDR 350 million before month-end, leaving IDR 78.4 million outstanding on the draw. The advance schedule shows IDR 75.6 million still to be applied under later claims. Keep the IDR 25.2 million retention item on its own schedule with the contract’s release trigger.

For a financial reporting illustration, assume the revised IDR 1,260 million is the transaction price for one performance obligation satisfied over time. Assume the accountant has verified that comparing the IDR 470 million of costs incurred with the IDR 940 million estimate faithfully measures progress and that both figures contain the appropriate costs. That implies 50% progress and IDR 630 million of cumulative revenue under the example’s assumptions. The IDR 504 million certificate follows the claim terms and sets the gross value approved for that draw. The accountant needs the advance and billing details, the rights in the contract and the delivery evidence to determine the balance sheet presentation. [IFRS 15, paragraphs 39–45, 105–108 and B18–B19](https://www.ifrs.org/content/dam/ifrs/publications/html-standards/english/2026/issued/ifrs15.html)

The bank has received IDR 476 million for the job, including the advance and the part-paid draw. If the contractor has paid IDR 380 million of job costs so far, job receipts exceed payments by IDR 96 million at this snapshot, before overheads and tax. The IDR 470 million of costs recorded includes IDR 55 million of supplier invoices still payable and the IDR 35 million accrual. Add those due items once to the forward cash forecast and plot their payment dates. Over the next four weeks, assuming IDR 120 million is the total paid and the only collection is the IDR 78.4 million, the net outflow is IDR 41.6 million. That leaves IDR 54.4 million of the original IDR 96 million if it was all still available. If collection moves beyond the window, the job needs at least IDR 24 million of additional funding before overheads and tax. The exact funding need depends on which week the bills fall due and what else the business must pay.

## Give retention and handover dates an owner

Set up a retention register by job, certificate and client, and keep subcontractor retention in a separate schedule. For each customer item, record the amount, contractual release event, evidence needed, expected submission date, likely collection and who will follow up. A contract may release part at acceptance and part after a defects period. A handover date on a programme is the site team’s expectation. The release calculation should use the evidence the agreement calls for, such as a signed acceptance or confirmation of the remediation work.

Review amounts that can be claimed alongside those waiting for a milestone. An old item can still be conditional, so check its current status before treating it as overdue. Once a claim for release is submitted, save the claim and monitor collection like any other customer balance. Ask your accountant whether the right to payment is already unconditional or still depends on a further event when reviewing how retention appears in the financial statements. Reconcile the register to the ledger and keep records of any agreed deductions or disputes. [IFRS 15, paragraphs 105–108](https://www.ifrs.org/content/dam/ifrs/publications/html-standards/english/2026/issued/ifrs15.html)

## Run a short project close and a weekly cash review

At the monthly close, agree a cut-off for receiving delivery dockets, timesheets, supplier invoices and subcontract valuations. Check actual job costs against the ledger, investigate any postings with missing site codes, and reconcile the commitment register against the pieces of work already delivered. Bring the job budget and variation log up to date. Then compare the progress record and billing schedule against the invoices actually sent, the amounts due and the receipts applied. Ask which differences are expected timing gaps and which ones need a decision from the project manager or client.

Give each active job a one-page review. Show original and revised contract values, costs to date, expected cost to complete, forecast margin, and changes since the last close. Include the next certification date, the largest payments coming due, overdue draws and upcoming retention releases. Name the person who owns each exception and write the date they expect to resolve it. The job file should allow a reviewer to pick any material figure and trace it back to an order, approval, measurement, invoice or bank transaction.

Revisit cash more frequently than the full close. Plot actual due dates for wages, supplier orders, subcontractor claims and deposits for the next few weeks. Compare them with the dates clients are likely to pay based on the state of each claim. Test at least one slippage scenario. If a draw could move past payroll day, agree who will follow up with the client and which expenses can move under the existing supplier terms. Look at all jobs together when deciding how much cash the business needs available. Several healthy jobs can require funding at the same time.

If you are setting up these records mid-project, start with one signed job, its approved changes and its most recent progress claim. Collect the open orders, invoices and payment records. Use those files to rebuild the cost forecast and find the next decision that needs better evidence. Once the team agrees what belongs in each record, repeat the same close on the next project.

A Bali yoga studio sells six class packs on Friday. A spa sends out gift vouchers during the same week. A retreat operator confirms a venue after collecting guest deposits. Each business has cash coming in and a service to deliver later. At month-end, the owner needs to know what the business earned, what it still owes customers and how much cash is available for commitments already made.

Start by connecting each sale to the payment, the service and any later adjustment. This works whether you run a small studio or sell classes, treatments and retreats through several channels. The records can show how many prepaid sessions are available for customers to use, which vouchers are still open and whether a retreat could fund refunds when they fall due.

## Give each transaction its own reference

List where your business takes bookings and money. A studio might use a booking platform, QRIS at reception and bank transfers for private sessions. A retreat team might also take card payments or accept deposits through a booking agent. Give each sale a reference and record what was purchased, who sold it, the date money was collected, the agreed service period and the original currency. Use the same reference for related refunds, credits or changes. An instructor’s attendance register can then be traced back to a customer booking without relying on someone’s memory at the end of the month.

Keep separate records of payments taken from customers and settlements received by the business. A processor can send several days of payments in a single bank credit after deducting fees and refunds. Retain the settlement report and match each line to its sale or adjustment. For a simple example, three IDR 500,000 payments make up an IDR 1,500,000 collection. If the provider deducts an illustrative IDR 12,000 fee and there are no other adjustments, the bank credit is IDR 1,488,000. The underlying record explains both the collection and the fee.

Check settlement status when an expected payout is missing. Xendit’s transaction report, for example, identifies payments awaiting settlement, settled payments and refunds. These fields help you carry an unsettled payment into the next period and follow up on it when the expected payout date passes. [Xendit’s transaction report](https://docs.xendit.co/docs/transactions-report)

Confirm which company sold the service when several businesses share a premises or brand. A spa inside a hotel may collect a guest’s payment itself or rely on the hotel to collect and settle an agreed amount. Save the agreement, booking reference and settlement statement together. For cash, use a counted drawer and record transfers to the bank, refunds paid from cash and any difference identified at closing. For international retreat bookings, retain the currency charged, the amount collected or converted and the fees shown on the account. Save the monthly exports before a team member clears old orders or changes software.

## Follow class packs from sale to redemption

Maintain a schedule of what each customer can still use. At a minimum, keep the package reference, number of visits purchased, amount paid, applicable terms, visits redeemed and remaining balance. Give extensions, cancellations and manual corrections a reason and an approval reference. A studio manager can use session counts to plan capacity, while the accountant needs the relevant amounts in rupiah. A class paid for with a package can appear in attendance and class revenue records with its payment method marked as redemption. That marker helps prevent the cash receipt from being counted a second time when the customer checks in.

For businesses applying Indonesia’s PSAK 115 or IFRS 15, a customer payment received before the promised service is supplied generally creates a contract liability. The business recognises revenue as it satisfies the corresponding obligations. Confirm which standards apply to your company and how they apply to each offer. [IAI’s current accounting standards index](https://web.iaiglobal.or.id/SAK-IAI/PSAK%20Umum?page_berita=1) · [IFRS 15, paragraphs 31 and 106 and Appendix B](https://eur-lex.europa.eu/legal-content/EN/TXT/?uri=CELEX%3A32016R1905)

For an accrual-based example, suppose a studio charges IDR 1,500,000 for ten identical visits and allocates IDR 150,000 to each visit. It records the advance as an obligation to provide the visits. Assume all packs follow the same terms and value per visit and the example excludes tax, fees and expired credits. The studio opens the month with 72 unused prepaid visits. It sells 60 more visits, delivers 68 and refunds four unused visits at their original value. Here is how the month rolls forward.

| Movement | Visits | Amount |
| --- | ---: | ---: |
| Opening prepaid balance | 72 | IDR 10,800,000 |
| New packs sold | 60 | IDR 9,000,000 |
| Visits delivered | (68) | (IDR 10,200,000) |
| Unused visits refunded | (4) | (IDR 600,000) |
| Closing prepaid balance | 60 | IDR 9,000,000 |

The packs contribute IDR 10,200,000 of earned revenue for visits delivered under these assumptions. The closing IDR 9,000,000 is supported by the 60 unused visits still recorded against customer accounts. The net cash collected on packs during the month is IDR 8,400,000 before processing fees. Compare the closing schedule with the ledger, match refunds to their original sales and carry the remaining visit detail into the following month. If one customer books a session on the last day and attends next month, keep the attendance and posting dates visible so your accountant can apply the correct cut-off.

Check what a visit status means in the system you use. A booking can reserve space before the customer attends, and a timely cancellation can return a credit. As one example, Mindbody’s Visits Remaining report distinguishes visits left on a package from those already reserved for future classes. Check the date filters and when attendance updates before accepting the export as a month-end balance. Use the booked detail for staffing and the visit history to check which services were delivered in the period you are closing. Keep no-shows and late cancellations identifiable so you can review how the studio applied its terms and whether a charge, released credit or other adjustment needs to be recorded. [Mindbody’s Visits Remaining report](https://support.mindbodyonline.com/s/article/203256803-Visits-Remaining-report?language=en_US)

Keep separate schedules if packs have different prices, included services or expiry rules. An introductory six-class offer can have a different value per visit from a 20-class package. A pack that includes classes and a private consultation may need its price allocated across distinct services under the accounting framework you use. Have your accountant confirm how availability, redemption, cancellations and expiry affect the reporting. Preserve the terms that applied at sale, particularly when a customer can freeze a pack, transfer it or use it at another location.

## Record memberships and spa vouchers on their own terms

A membership can give someone access throughout a month, a fixed number of benefits or a mix of access and credits. List the period covered by each charge, the start and end dates, and any freeze or cancellation. Review renewals taken before the period begins and amounts still due for active memberships. A sold-out class can have 18 people in the room, including drop-ins, pack redemptions and members. Keep those groups identifiable. The class roster helps explain attendance and instructor costs, while the membership agreements and billing records support revenue.

Consider a member who pays on the 25th for access that starts on the first day of the following month. The current bank statement shows the payment. The membership history needs to show the period covered so the person closing the books can assess the treatment of the advance. If a membership is frozen after the charge, preserve the date the freeze takes effect, the revised service period and any credit issued. Use the same dates in the customer account and monthly schedule. A change made only in the booking calendar can leave the finance records following the original terms.

For spa vouchers, keep a register that records the code, sale date, purchaser reference when available, amount paid, terms, issuer and redeemed balance. Track whether it buys a specific treatment or carries a monetary value that a customer can use across services. A gift voucher paid for by a customer should remain distinguishable from a promotional code given away as a discount. Where partial redemption is allowed, link each use to the original code and retain the remaining balance. Mindbody’s Gift Cards report, for example, shows sales and unredeemed amounts by card. Record the face value and the amount the customer actually paid when a voucher is sold at a discount. A voucher redeemed at another location also needs enough detail to settle any amount due between the businesses involved. [Mindbody’s Gift Cards report](https://support.mindbodyonline.com/s/article/203257113-Gift-Cards-report?language=en_US)

When a package, membership credit or voucher expires in the booking system, retain the history and review the terms before adjusting the accounts. Check whether your policies allow an extension, credit transfer or discretionary reinstatement and confirm who has approved past changes. Use aged balances to investigate missing redemptions and to estimate future service demand. Under IFRS 15, recognising revenue for unused non-refundable rights depends on entitlement, patterns of redemption or whether further use is remote, as applicable. Ask your accountant to review the evidence before recognising unused amounts. [IFRS 15, paragraphs B44–B47](https://eur-lex.europa.eu/legal-content/EN/TXT/?uri=CELEX%3A32016R1905)

## Plan retreat cash around the dates on the contracts

Give every retreat a code that runs through guest invoices, booking payments, venue agreements, facilitator contracts, supplier bills and refunds. Keep each guest’s deposit, balance due, amounts paid, cancellation terms and refund status together. For each supplier, record what has been committed, when payment is due, and how much the supplier would return if guest numbers fall or the event is cancelled. Keep the guest and supplier cut-offs in the same forecast. They can fall on different days and the cash impact is often most severe in the gap between them.

Suppose 16 participants each pay a deposit of IDR 2,500,000. The retreat has collected IDR 40,000,000. The operator pays IDR 12,000,000 to reserve the venue and IDR 3,000,000 to secure a facilitator. That leaves IDR 25,000,000 of the retreat’s collections in the bank before other activity. The guest terms allow a full deposit refund through the end of the month. In the cancellation scenario, the retreat is called off, the unpaid supplier balances are waived under the agreements and all guest refunds fall due on the same day. The IDR 40,000,000 payable exceeds current retreat cash by IDR 15,000,000. Assume the venue would return IDR 8,000,000 only after guest refunds are due and the facilitator would make no repayment. The cash gap is still IDR 15,000,000 on the refund date and would fall to IDR 7,000,000 once the venue refund arrives.

Now model a scenario in which three people request refunds and the retreat proceeds. Refunding IDR 7,500,000 leaves IDR 17,500,000 from the balance above. Assume the venue balance of IDR 9,000,000, facilitator balance of IDR 5,000,000 and a transport commitment of IDR 4,000,000 fall due before the remaining participants’ final payments settle. Those bills total IDR 18,000,000. In that scenario the retreat is short IDR 500,000 until more cash clears, even before any provision for extra costs. Date each inflow according to when money is expected to reach your account. Update the forecast when the booking team changes guest terms or the venue agrees to a new payment date.

Keep a weekly view until the event’s commitments and refunds clear. A repeatable forecast begins with cash currently attributable to the retreat, adds scheduled receipts, then deducts due refunds and supplier payments by week. Assign someone to follow each large outstanding guest balance and confirm each supplier due date. Include possible foreign exchange or payment fees as estimates with a source or assumption. If the retreat shares an account with the rest of your business, reconcile the retreat schedule to your bank movements and confirm which funds are available to meet obligations across the business.

## Match instructor and venue costs to the right activity

Record instructor payment terms before classes or events begin. A teacher might earn a flat amount per class, a per-person fee or a share of qualifying receipts. A retreat facilitator might receive a deposit and a balance once the event is delivered. Ask the contract owner to define which bookings count toward a variable fee and how the calculation handles trial classes, refunds and no-shows. Save the attendance report, calculation and approval for each payment period. Where somebody covers a late shift or session, update the roster and identify who approved the substitution.

Review supplier commitments alongside invoices. An invoice received after the retreat can cover services delivered during it, while a payment before the retreat might be a deposit for a future service. Keep the payment date, service date and contract reference visible for the month-end review. For shared costs, use a consistent allocation you can explain. Studio rent might follow scheduled room hours across two activities, while an exclusive retreat venue belongs to its event. Keep deposits recoverable under a contract on a schedule the team can review until they are applied, returned or otherwise resolved.

Use the records to compare offerings. Start with earned revenue for a class series, treatment or retreat, then identify the costs directly tied to delivering it. A popular class with IDR 900,000 of earned revenue and IDR 450,000 in instructor and other direct costs contributes IDR 450,000 before shared overhead. If another session earns IDR 750,000 and incurs IDR 250,000 of direct costs, it contributes IDR 500,000 on the same basis. Track room capacity, attendance and repeat bookings separately so financial and scheduling decisions have the context they need.

## Close the month with evidence you can hand over

Ask reception, booking staff and the retreat lead to submit a consistent monthly set of records. Include booking and attendance reports, membership and voucher balances, provider settlements, bank statements, instructor cost calculations, supplier invoices and the list of unpaid guest or corporate bookings. Capture refund approvals and any changes to terms alongside the original references. Run the reports through the agreed cut-off, name the files by period and keep the original exports. Then anyone reviewing a disputed amount can get from a line in the ledger to the records that explain it.

Pick a reviewer for each remaining difference. Reception can confirm whether a package was used. The events lead can confirm a venue balance or follow up on a missed payment. Your accountant can review unusual sales terms, balances and tax-sensitive transactions. Bring them the accepted agreements, receipts, service descriptions and breakdowns by location or activity, especially where one customer price includes accommodation, meals and sessions. The accounting and reporting treatment depends on the contract, the services supplied and the applicable framework. Confirm the relevant obligations and evidence before preparing any filing.

End the close with balances for open credits, prepaid packages and outstanding retreat payments, backed by customer and supplier detail. Carry unresolved items forward with an amount, owner and next action. Review the next retreat’s cash forecast and the volume of studio or spa services yet to be delivered before committing to a new event or adding capacity.
